Reviewing is the work now.

Making a design got cheap. Knowing whether it should ship did not. Notes from a year of letting product owners design with AI.

Every day, product owners at snelstart ask a skill for a design. Most of the time they get one within minutes. It usually looks fine.

That last part is the problem. A bad design is easy to catch. Everyone sees it. A design that looks fine is harder. It uses the right components, the spacing is right, the copy is friendly. And it solves a slightly different problem from the one the user has.

Producing the work is not the hard bit anymore. Reviewing it is.

What a review actually checks

When I look at a proposal, I check three things, in this order of difficulty.

Does it use what we have? The right component, the right token, the right tone of voice. This is the part AI is good at checking, as long as the rules are written down. So we wrote them down, in skills that carry the design system and tone of voice.

Does it answer the question that was asked? Often the question in the ticket is not the real question. A PO asks for a filter. The user wants to find one invoice. Those lead to different screens.

Does it fit the person using it? What are they in the middle of? What do they already know? What happens if they get it wrong? This is the part that needs someone who has sat next to users. It is also the part a generated design is most confident about and least likely to get right.

Design the review, not just the screen

If reviewing is the work, then the review needs to be designed like anything else. Who decides? When? With what in front of them?

For us that became a simple flow. A PO talks the problem through with the skill first. Small questions end in a proposal, and a proposal is not a decision: it waits on a dashboard until a designer approves it. Bigger questions go straight to a designer, with the ticket and the conversation attached, so the designer starts from the problem and not from a meeting.

The most important thing the skill does is say "this needs a designer". A tool that always has an answer is the risky kind.

The same question, everywhere

The longer I work like this, the more I see the same pattern in the products I design. A system proposes. A person decides. A bank transaction matched to an invoice. A KYC file flagged for review. A design proposal from a skill.

In each case the design work is the same. Make it quick to see whether the proposal is right. Put the doubtful cases first, not the ones that are fine. Make stepping in cheap, one action instead of a form.

What I haven't solved

Approval can turn into clicking. If a designer approves twenty proposals a day and nineteen are fine, the twentieth gets the same two seconds as the rest. That is the real risk of this way of working, and it is a design problem too: what should a reviewer see first, and how do you keep the review from becoming a habit?

I don't have a full answer yet. I do know it won't come from making the proposals faster.

What I'd tell another designer

Don't race AI on speed. It will win. The skill that got more valuable is the one that was always the hard part: knowing what good looks like for these people, in this product, and saying no when something isn't it.

— Nadja
